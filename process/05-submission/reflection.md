## What worked

The most useful thing I did was make a bad version on purpose. The baseline, one sentence and my résumé, came back looking finished: real facts, clean layout, nothing embarrassing. That was the point. Once I had it next to my September 3 page, the problem was obvious. Both of them were my résumé in a browser, and a recruiter already has my résumé.

Writing a brief before building was the turn. Audience, the role I want, four case files in priority order, and a list of things the site should never do. Naming the things to avoid mattered as much as naming what to build: gradients, skill bars, "passionate about", a page where everything is centred. Those are the tells that make a page look machine-made, and a model won't avoid them unless you say so.

Running that same brief through a second tool showed me the limit of that. Gemini avoided all twelve banned patterns and still wrote like a machine — "Visual Constitution", "lab-grade", "bare-metal embedded work". A list of banned words changes the vocabulary, not the register. If I wanted plain sentences I would have had to ask for plain sentences.

Keeping a version history helped more than I expected. Tagging the baseline, the first build and the post-review version meant the progress was visible instead of asserted. [ME: one line on whether that felt like overhead or not]

## What I would do differently

I would gather inspiration before prompting, not after. I picked a direction from three written options rather than from things I had actually seen and liked, which is backwards from how the professor framed it in class.

I would also collect evidence earlier. Three of my four case files have no image, because the screenshots and artifacts live on machines and in folders I didn't pull together in time. The work is real; the proof of it isn't in the site yet.

## Best practices I'm taking with me

1. **Write the brief before the prompt.** Audience, goal, priority order, constraints. Everything the baseline got wrong came from those four things being missing.
2. **Tell it what not to do.** The list of banned patterns did more for the look than any styling instruction.
3. **Check the facts, every time.** The baseline invented a team for a solo project and a preference I never stated. Later, the refined build invented "I answer email quickly" on its own. Same mistake, different model, and I only caught it because I was reading for it.
4. **Make the process the evidence.** Commits, tags, an AI log and a decision log cost almost nothing while working and are the only proof afterwards that the result came from decisions rather than luck.
5. **Have something else review it.** The tool that wrote the site is the worst judge of it.
6. **Run the same prompt twice, in two tools.** It's the cheapest way to see which parts of the result came from my brief and which came from the model's habits. Both tools built the same page structure, which told me the brief was doing its job.
7. **Check that the attachment actually went through.** I pasted the prompt into Gemini without the résumé. The prompt's first line says the résumé is attached and is the only source of facts about me, and it built the site anyway — ten honest TODOs and six invented details, including an email address I've never used. A missing input didn't stop it and didn't make it sound any less sure of itself. That's the thing I'll be watching for from now on.
